\# SOUND DESIGN



Sound design in motion design is not a collection of effects added after animation.



It is part of the motion language.



For product films, SaaS launches, application demos, AI videos, cinematic UI, social videos, feature showcases, and brand films, sound should help the viewer:



1\. perceive movement;

2\. understand hierarchy;

3\. anticipate events;

4\. feel impact;

5\. distinguish interaction from decoration;

6\. understand transitions;

7\. perceive scale, material, depth, and speed;

8\. experience the video as one coherent audiovisual system.



The objective is not:



```text

ADD SOUND TO EVERYTHING

```



The objective is:



```text

DESIGN WHAT THE VIEWER SHOULD HEAR

AND WHAT THE VIEWER SHOULD NOT HEAR

```



Silence is part of sound design.



\---



\# CORE PRINCIPLE



Every sound must answer:



```text

WHAT VISUAL OR NARRATIVE EVENT DOES THIS SOUND SERVE?

```



Then:



```text

WHY SHOULD IT BE HEARD?

WHAT INFORMATION DOES IT ADD?

HOW IMPORTANT IS THE EVENT?

WHAT SONIC FAMILY FITS IT?

HOW LOUD SHOULD IT FEEL RELATIVE TO THE MIX?

WHEN SHOULD ITS PERCEPTUAL PEAK OCCUR?

HOW LONG SHOULD ITS TAIL REMAIN?

SHOULD IT EXIST AT ALL?

```



Never begin with:



```text

"What SFX can I add here?"

```



Begin with:



```text

"What deserves sonic attention?"

```



\---



\# SOUND DESIGN DECISION CHAIN



Use:



```text

MESSAGE

→ VISUAL EVENT

→ EVENT IMPORTANCE

→ SONIC PURPOSE

→ SOUND FAMILY

→ MATERIAL / CHARACTER

→ TIMING

→ LAYER

→ LEVEL

→ DENSITY

→ TAIL

→ MIX RELATIONSHIP

→ RENDER VALIDATION

```



Sound follows communication.



Not decoration.



\---



\# 1. SONIC INTENT



Before selecting sounds, define the sonic intention.



Possible intentions:



```text

PREMIUM

TECHNICAL

FUTURISTIC

PHYSICAL

MINIMAL

PLAYFUL

CINEMATIC

EDITORIAL

ENERGETIC

SOFT

MECHANICAL

ORGANIC

DIGITAL

HYBRID

```



This intention should agree with:



```text

art direction

motion personality

product identity

brand

camera language

music

editing rhythm

```



Do not create a premium visual system and cover it with arcade-like UI bleeps unless the contrast is deliberate.



\---



\# 2. NARRATIVE FUNCTION



Sound can perform different narrative jobs.



Classify each cue.



```text

ACTION

CONFIRMATION

TRANSITION

ANTICIPATION

IMPACT

ATMOSPHERE

CONTINUITY

EMPHASIS

MATERIAL

SPATIAL INFORMATION

BRAND

RHYTHM

```



One sound may perform multiple jobs, but it should have a primary purpose.



\---



\# ACTION



Sound describes something actually happening.



Examples:



```text

click

switch

typing

card landing

object passing camera

panel opening

shutter

mechanical movement

```



When the action is recognizable, prefer a sound that respects its physical or designed character.



\---



\# CONFIRMATION



Sound confirms that an action completed.



Examples:



```text

successful selection

toggle enabled

workflow completed

AI result generated

upload finished

connection established

```



Confirmation should not automatically mean:



```text

generic success bleep

```



Choose a sonic language consistent with the product.



\---



\# TRANSITION



Sound helps connect scenes or states.



Examples:



```text

whoosh

sweep

pass-by

reverse texture

air movement

tonal bridge

J-cut

L-cut

```



A transition sound should reinforce the visual transition.



It should not exist only because there is a cut.



\---



\# ANTICIPATION



Sound can prepare the viewer before the visual event.



Examples:



```text

riser

reverse impact

increasing texture

filter opening

building ambience

musical pickup

```



Anticipation creates expectation.



Use it before meaningful events, not every reveal.



\---



\# IMPACT



Impact communicates arrival, collision, emphasis, completion, or scale.



Examples:



```text

hero object lands

headline hits

device locks into position

major metric appears

logo resolves

drop occurs

```



Impact strength must match event importance.



\---



\# ATMOSPHERE



Atmosphere creates continuity and space.



Examples:



```text

soft room tone

digital hum

machine ambience

environment

air

projector texture

subtle tonal bed

```



Atmosphere may be nearly unnoticed.



Its purpose can be to prevent the scene from feeling sonically empty.



\---



\# CONTINUITY



Sound can connect two shots even when the picture changes.



This is especially important for:



```text

J-cuts

L-cuts

ambient beds

music

long reverb tails

sound bridges

```



Picture and audio do not need to cut on the same frame.



\---



\# 3. SOUND HIERARCHY



Not every sound has equal importance.



Define:



```text

PRIMARY

SECONDARY

TERTIARY

BED

```



Example:



```text

HERO IMPACT

&#x20;   ↓

PRIMARY



TRANSITION WHOOSH

&#x20;   ↓

SECONDARY



UI CLICK

&#x20;   ↓

TERTIARY



AMBIENCE

&#x20;   ↓

BED

```



Hierarchy can be expressed through:



```text

level

frequency

duration

brightness

stereo width

density

transient strength

timing

silence around the sound

```



\---



\# IMPORTANCE → LOUDNESS



Do not use one volume for every SFX.



The importance of the event should influence its perceived prominence.



But:



```text

VOLUME VALUE ≠ PERCEIVED LOUDNESS

```



A quiet source at `volume={1}` can remain quieter than a loud source at `volume={0.3}`.



Always consider source level.



\---



\# 4. THE SONIC LAYER SYSTEM



Think in layers:



```text

MUSIC

AMBIENCE

MOVEMENT

INTERACTION

TRANSITION

IMPACT

DETAIL

BRAND

```



Not every scene needs every layer.



\---



\# MUSIC LAYER



Music provides:



```text

energy

rhythm

emotional direction

structure

continuity

```



Music is not automatically the loudest or most important layer at every moment.



Allow important sound-design events to emerge.



\---



\# AMBIENCE LAYER



Ambience provides a sonic environment.



Examples:



```text

air

hum

room tone

machine texture

digital atmosphere

environment

```



Beds may extend for the entire shot.



They should usually remain subordinate.



\---



\# MOVEMENT LAYER



Movement sounds describe:



```text

camera push

camera pull

fly-by

panel travel

object movement

fast wipe

depth pass

```



Typical families include:



```text

whoosh

swoosh

air movement

swipe

pass-by

```



The sound's shape should match the motion's:



```text

direction

speed

scale

acceleration

```



\---



\# INTERACTION LAYER



Interaction sounds belong to deliberate actions.



Examples:



```text

mouse click

keyboard

toggle

switch

tap

selection

notification

confirmation

```



Use them when the interaction matters.



Do not voice every tiny UI event.



\---



\# TRANSITION LAYER



Transition sounds help bridge scenes.



They may:



```text

lead into the next shot

trail from the previous shot

peak on the cut

bridge two visual states

```



Use:



```text

J-cut

L-cut

whoosh

riser

tail

audio match

```



according to editorial intent.



\---



\# IMPACT LAYER



Impact marks major moments.



Possible sounds:



```text

hit

slam

snap

bass hit

sub impact

mechanical lock

shutter

designed transient

```



Reserve strong impacts for strong events.



If everything hits, nothing hits.



\---



\# DETAIL LAYER



Small sounds can make interfaces and motion feel tangible.



Examples:



```text

tiny click

soft tick

paper texture

micro movement

small pop

subtle mechanical sound

```



These should not compete with primary cues.



\---



\# BRAND LAYER



Brand sound may accompany:



```text

logo

brand reveal

end card

product identity

sonic signature

```



A brand cue should feel intentional and repeatable.



Avoid attaching a random sparkle to every logo.



\---



\# 5. SFX VOCABULARY



A practical core vocabulary for product motion includes:



```text

WHOOSH

camera/object movement



IMPACT

landing/emphasis



RISER

build/anticipation



SPARKLE

afterglow/detail



CLICK

interaction



TRANSITION

scene change

```



This is a vocabulary, not a mandatory checklist.



The detailed catalog belongs in:



```text

references/audio/sfx-language.md

```



\---



\# 6. MATERIAL LANGUAGE



Whenever possible, sounds should imply material.



Ask:



```text

WHAT WOULD THIS FEEL LIKE IF IT WERE PHYSICAL?

```



Possible material languages:



```text

GLASS

METAL

PAPER

PLASTIC

AIR

DIGITAL

MECHANICAL

ELECTRICAL

SOFT / FABRIC

LIQUID

HYBRID

```



For cinematic UI, a real or foley-like material can often feel more sophisticated than generic game-interface feedback.



Example:



```text

visual:

mechanical switch



better direction:

physical switch texture



possible weaker direction:

generic confirm-bleep

```



unless the brand intentionally uses synthetic feedback.



\---



\# PHYSICAL + DESIGNED HYBRID



A strong product-video sound language can combine:



```text

REAL / FOLEY CHARACTER

\+

DESIGNED AIR / TONAL LAYER

```



Example:



```text

soft physical click

\+

subtle airy transient

```



This can preserve tangibility while maintaining polish.



\---



\# 7. DENSITY



Sound density should follow visual and narrative density.



Possible states:



```text

SPARSE

MODERATE

DENSE

```



A sparse visual moment may need only:



```text

music

\+

one important SFX

```



A high-energy assembly may support:



```text

music

\+

movement

\+

interactions

\+

impact

\+

detail

```



But density must remain readable.



\---



\# SOUND DENSITY RULE



Do not ask:



```text

"How many sounds can fit?"

```



Ask:



```text

"How many sonic events can the viewer meaningfully perceive?"

```



\---



\# 8. SILENCE



Silence is an active design tool.



Use silence or reduced density to:



```text

create anticipation

make an impact feel larger

separate sections

reset attention

emphasize a message

create premium restraint

prepare a logo

```



A breakdown can be defined as much by removed layers as by added layers.



\---



\# CONTRAST



Sound requires contrast.



```text

quiet → loud

dense → sparse

build → release

motion → stop

ambience → silence

riser → impact

impact → tail

```



Without contrast, constant intensity becomes flat.



\---



\# 9. ENERGY CURVE



Sound should follow the video's energy architecture.



Example:



```text

INTRO

low density



BUILD

additional layers



FEATURE

rhythmic detail



HERO MOMENT

largest impact



BREAKDOWN

reduced layers



CTA

controlled resolution

```



Do not make every shot sonically maximal.



\---



\# 10. SONIC PHRASES



Individual SFX can form phrases.



A useful product-motion phrase:



```text

RISER

→ IMPACT

→ SPARKLE

```



Interpretation:



```text

RISER

anticipation / build



IMPACT

arrival / landing



SPARKLE

afterglow / detail

```



Do not apply this sequence mechanically to every shot.



Use it for meaningful assembly or reveal moments.



\---



\# 11. SOUND AND MOVEMENT



Movement sound should correspond to the motion envelope.



Think:



```text

MOTION START

MOTION ACCELERATION

MOTION PEAK

MOTION DECELERATION

MOTION LANDING

```



The sound may correspond to one or several of these phases.



Example:



```text

camera push

→ whoosh builds

→ peak near maximum perceived movement

→ tail resolves as camera settles

```



\---



\# 12. SOUND AND CAMERA



Camera movement can have sonic weight.



Possible mapping:



```text

PUSH-IN

→ forward air / rising texture



PULL-OUT

→ release / widening texture



WHIP

→ fast whoosh



DEPTH PASS

→ pass-by



HARD CAMERA LAND

→ impact



SLOW FLOAT

→ ambience / subtle movement texture

```



Do not sonify every camera movement.



Only voice movements that benefit from sonic reinforcement.



\---



\# 13. SOUND AND UI



UI sound should communicate:



```text

interaction

state change

confirmation

importance

```



Potential mappings:



```text

BUTTON PRESS

→ click / tactile transient



TOGGLE

→ mechanical switch / small snap



CARD APPEAR

→ subtle pop / air



NOTIFICATION

→ restrained notification texture



SUCCESS

→ confirmation



DATA UPDATE

→ subtle tick / sequence



AI RESULT

→ designed completion texture

```



Avoid turning a professional SaaS interface into a game interface.



\---



\# 14. RECOGNIZABLE ACTIONS



Recognizable actions often benefit from recognizable sonic cues.



Examples:



```text

typing

clicking

switching

landing

shattering

shutter

```



But:



```text

RECOGNIZABLE ≠ MUST BE LOUD

```



A cue can remain subtle.



\---



\# 15. REPEATED EVENTS



Repeated identical events should not sound like a machine gun unless that is intentional.



For repeated UI events:



```text

rotate variants

vary level slightly

vary timing when appropriate

avoid identical adjacent samples

```



Possible approach:



```text

2–4 variants

\+

small loudness variation

```



The goal is natural variation.



Not random chaos.



\---



\# 16. TIMING PHILOSOPHY



There is no universal rule such as:



```text

ALL SFX MUST BE 3 FRAMES EARLY

```



or:



```text

ALL SFX MUST BEGIN EXACTLY ON THE VISUAL FRAME

```



Different sound structures require different alignment strategies.



Use:



```text

PERCEPTUAL SYNC

```



\---



\# 17. PERCEPTUAL SYNC



A sound has multiple timing landmarks:



```text

FILE START

AUDIBLE ONSET

TRANSIENT

PEAK

BODY

TAIL

```



A visual event also has landmarks:



```text

ANIMATION START

FIRST VISIBLE FRAME

MAXIMUM VELOCITY

VISUAL PEAK

CONTACT / LANDING

SETTLE

```



Synchronize the relevant landmarks.



Not merely the file start and animation start.



\---



\# APPEARING ELEMENTS



For:



```text

cards

chips

banners

windows

text

```



the audible onset often belongs near the first frame where the element is clearly perceptible.



The layer in-point may be too early when the element begins from:



```text

opacity 0

scale 0

off-screen

blurred invisibility

```



Inspect the actual render.



\---



\# CLICKS



For a click:



```text

audible onset

→ pressed-state frame

```



The reaction may occur immediately after.



The important moment is the perceived interaction.



\---



\# WHOOSH



For:



```text

fly-by

wipe

camera move

fast object travel

```



align the sound's perceptual peak with the visual movement peak.



For a hard transition, the peak may belong on the cut.



\---



\# IMPACT



For a landing:



```text

impact transient

→ visual contact / landing

```



However, perception and encoding may require small adjustments.



Verify in the final render.



\---



\# 18. EARLY / EXACT / LATE



A sound may:



```text

LEAD

LAND

TRAIL

```



the picture.



These are different creative relationships.



\---



\# LEAD



Sound occurs before the visual event.



Purpose:



```text

anticipation

pull viewer forward

prepare reveal

increase perceived responsiveness

```



Examples:



```text

J-cut

riser

reverse texture

```



\---



\# LAND



Sound and visual event perceptually coincide.



Purpose:



```text

impact

confirmation

physical contact

precision

```



Examples:



```text

click

landing

snap

major metric

```



\---



\# TRAIL



Sound continues after the visual event.



Purpose:



```text

smooth transition

preserve continuity

allow reverb

create afterglow

```



Examples:



```text

L-cut

reverb tail

sparkle

ambience

```



\---



\# 19. J-CUT



A J-cut allows the next shot's audio to begin before its picture.



Concept:



```text

PICTURE:

AAAAAAA | BBBBBBB



AUDIO:

AAAA BBBBBBBBBBB

&#x20;      ↑

audio B leads

```



Useful for:



```text

reveals

dialogue

anticipation

pulling the viewer forward

```



Typical frame offsets found in source workflows may fall around several frames, but treat them as contextual starting points rather than universal constants.



\---



\# 20. L-CUT



An L-cut allows the current shot's audio to continue after the picture changes.



Concept:



```text

PICTURE:

AAAAAAA | BBBBBBB



AUDIO:

AAAAAAAAAAAA BBB

&#x20;           ↑

audio A trails

```



Useful for:



```text

smooth scene changes

ambience

dialogue

continuity

CTA resolution

```



\---



\# 21. AUDIO MATCH



A sound can bridge a cut.



Examples:



```text

same mechanical sound

same tonal texture

continuous ambience

shared impact tail

```



This creates an audio match even when the visual content changes.



\---



\# 22. TAILS



Do not cut every sound exactly when the visual action ends.



Some sounds need natural decay.



Especially:



```text

impacts

reverb

sparkle

ambience

resonance

```



A hard cut can make an impact feel unnaturally dry.



\---



\# LONG SAMPLES



Long samples should be controlled according to their function.



```text

MOVEMENT SOUND

→ trim to action when appropriate



BED / LOOP

→ extend through shot



IMPACT WITH REVERB

→ allow natural decay



TRANSITION

→ shape around transition

```



Do not edit solely by waveform duration.



Edit by narrative purpose.



\---



\# 23. VOLUME IS LAYERING



Volume is relational.



A numeric value alone tells little about perceived loudness.



Example source workflow may use ranges such as:



```text

music bed

≈ restrained



SFX

≈ wider working range

```



but these values depend heavily on source material.



Do not treat numeric multipliers as loudness standards.



\---



\# SOURCE LEVEL MATTERS



Consider:



```text

SOURCE PEAK

\+

SOURCE RMS / SHORT-TERM ENERGY

\+

TIMELINE GAIN

\+

MIX CONTEXT

=

PERCEIVED RESULT

```



A quiet file at maximum multiplier may still disappear beneath music.



A hot file at a small multiplier may dominate.



\---



\# 24. LEVEL CALIBRATION



Prefer calibration against the project's actual mix.



When possible:



```text

1\. inspect existing approved SFX;

2\. measure their levels;

3\. use them as project references;

4\. place new sounds relative to those references;

5\. export;

6\. measure again;

7\. listen.

```



Project context is more valuable than arbitrary universal values.



\---



\# 25. PEAKS AND LOUDNESS



Useful measurements can include:



```text

peak

true peak

short-window RMS

integrated loudness

transient peaks

```



Different sounds require different metrics.



A click and an ambience bed should not be evaluated identically.



\---



\# TRANSIENT-HEAVY SOUNDS



For:



```text

typing

keyboard

clicks

rustles

sharp UI sounds

```



average RMS can hide individual spikes.



Inspect transient behavior.



A single spike may be much louder than the average energy suggests.



\---



\# 26. FINAL PEAK SAFETY



For final delivery, check true peak.



A practical delivery target often keeps true peak below clipping, with approximately:



```text

≤ -1 dBTP

```



used in many workflows as safety margin.



Final requirements depend on delivery platform/specification.



If VO + music create overs, solve the mix appropriately.



If an individual SFX causes the spike, fix the SFX.



\---



\# 27. SOUND PRIORITY



When music is already dense, sound effects should often become more restrained.



Ask:



```text

WHAT DOES THE MUSIC ALREADY COMMUNICATE?

```



If the kick already creates the impact:



```text

do not automatically stack another huge impact

```



unless additional weight is required.



Sound design should complement music, not fight it.



\---



\# 28. FREQUENCY SPACE



Think about spectral competition.



Simplified regions:



```text

LOW

weight / impact / bass



MID

body / mechanical / many UI textures



HIGH

detail / sparkle / click / air

```



If music already occupies a region heavily, adding more energy there can create clutter.



Use frequency character as part of hierarchy.



\---



\# 29. LOW-FREQUENCY IMPACT



Low-frequency energy can create scale.



Use carefully for:



```text

hero landing

major transition

logo lock

large device

major metric

```



Do not add bass hits to every interaction.



\---



\# 30. HIGH-FREQUENCY DETAIL



High-frequency sounds can communicate:



```text

precision

detail

sparkle

digital character

small interaction

```



Excessive bright detail quickly becomes tiring.



\---



\# 31. SOUND AND TYPOGRAPHY



Typography can receive sound when the text event matters.



Possible relationships:



```text

HEADLINE LAND

→ impact



WORD EMPHASIS

→ small accent



MASK REVEAL

→ soft movement sound



TYPE-ON

→ typing texture



HIGHLIGHT

→ subtle tick / sparkle



CTA

→ restrained confirmation

```



Do not sound every word.



Use:



```text

references/typography.md

```



to determine text hierarchy first.



\---



\# 32. SOUND AND TRANSITIONS



Transition sound should follow transition mechanics.



Examples:



```text

WHIP

→ fast whoosh



DEPTH PUSH

→ forward air / low rise



OBJECT WIPE

→ pass-by



MATCH CUT

→ shared sound / continuous texture



HARD CUT

→ often no transition SFX required



FADE

→ ambience / tonal continuity



FLASH

→ sharp transient only if justified

```



A hard cut is allowed to remain hard.



\---



\# 33. SOUND AND FEATURE FOCUS



When highlighting a product feature:



```text

FEATURE ENTERS

→ subtle cue



FOCUS / ZOOM

→ movement cue if useful



ACTION

→ interaction sound



RESULT

→ confirmation / impact

```



Do not create four loud sounds for one small feature.



Use hierarchy.



\---



\# 34. SOUND AND DATA FLOW



For:



```text

nodes

agents

connections

automations

data packets

integrations

```



sound can reinforce direction.



Possible mapping:



```text

NODE ACTIVATION

→ tick / pulse



CONNECTION

→ link sound



DATA TRAVEL

→ movement texture



ARRIVAL

→ confirmation



SYSTEM COMPLETION

→ resolved impact / tonal lock

```



Avoid a sound on every particle.



Sound semantic events, not decorative particles.



\---



\# 35. SOUND AND AI



Avoid generic "AI sound" clichés unless appropriate.



AI can be represented through:



```text

precision

layered texture

soft computation

subtle digital movement

tonal evolution

controlled glitches

data pulses

```



Do not automatically use:



```text

random glitch

robot bleep

sci-fi alarm

```



AI product sound should match the product's brand.



\---



\# 36. CINEMATIC UI SOUND



Cinematic UI often works best when combining:



```text

physical tactility

\+

designed movement

\+

controlled digital detail

```



Example:



```text

realistic click

\+

airy micro-whoosh

\+

subtle confirmation tone

```



But use only the layers needed.



\---



\# 37. SOUND AND LOGO



Logo sound should function as a sonic lock.



Possible structure:



```text

ANTICIPATION

→ LOGO ARRIVAL

→ SONIC RESOLUTION

```



Or simply:



```text

LOGO ARRIVAL

→ SINGLE CLEAN BRAND CUE

```



The logo does not require:



```text

riser

\+ giant impact

\+ sparkle

\+ bleep

\+ bass

```



unless the creative direction supports it.



\---



\# 38. SOUND AND CTA



CTA sound should support closure.



Possible approaches:



```text

no SFX

soft confirmation

tonal resolution

music resolution

ambient tail

```



Give the CTA sonic breathing room.



\---



\# 39. SOUND VARIETY



Repeated sounds should maintain family resemblance without exact repetition.



Variation dimensions:



```text

sample

pitch

level

length

brightness

timing

```



Keep variation subtle.



The viewer should perceive coherence, not randomness.



\---



\# 40. AUDIO ASSET SELECTION



When selecting assets, evaluate:



```text

material

transient

length

tail

brightness

noise

frequency balance

source level

stereo character

brand fit

```



Do not choose by filename alone.



A file called:



```text

whoosh\_01.wav

```



does not automatically fit the motion.



\---



\# 41. ASSET AUDITION



When the system cannot reliably judge the aesthetic character by hearing, separate:



```text

OBJECTIVE ANALYSIS

from

SUBJECTIVE SELECTION

```



Objective analysis may determine:



```text

onset

peak

tail

duration

level

brightness

```



Human audition may determine:



```text

taste

brand fit

emotional quality

material feel

```



Do not pretend numerical analysis replaces listening.



\---



\# 42. AUDIO IMPLEMENTATION — REMOTION



Use:



```tsx

import {Audio} from '@remotion/media';

```



Basic:



```tsx

<Audio src={staticFile('audio/sound.wav')} />

```



Multiple `<Audio>` elements can create layered sound.



\---



\# AUDIO PLACEMENT



Audio can be positioned using frame timing.



Example:



```tsx

<Audio

&#x20; from={startFrame}

&#x20; src={staticFile('sfx/impact.wav')}

/>

```



Treat timing as part of the shot system.



Avoid unexplained bare frame numbers when a shot-relative value can be used.



\---



\# SHOT-RELATIVE AUDIO



Prefer:



```text

SHOT START

\+

EVENT OFFSET

```



over unrelated global magic numbers.



Concept:



```tsx

const cueFrame = SHOTS.hero.from + 48;

```



This keeps sound attached to the action when shots move.



\---



\# AUDIO TRIMMING



Use appropriate trimming and duration controls to align audio with action.



For long movement sounds:



```text

action duration

→ audio duration

```



For impacts:



```text

preserve tail when useful

```



\---



\# AUDIO VOLUME



Remotion supports static and animated volume.



Example:



```tsx

<Audio

&#x20; src={staticFile('audio/music.mp3')}

&#x20; volume={0.3}

/>

```



For procedural fades:



```tsx

<Audio

&#x20; src={staticFile('audio/music.mp3')}

&#x20; volume={(mediaFrame) =>

&#x20;   interpolate(mediaFrame, \[0, 30], \[0, 1], {

&#x20;     extrapolateLeft: 'clamp',

&#x20;     extrapolateRight: 'clamp',

&#x20;   })

&#x20; }

/>

```



Do not confuse the multiplier with a target dB level.

\# 45. AUDIO TRUTH

```text

DESIGNED TIMING

and
```

```text

FINAL FILE GUARANTEED CORRECT

```

\# 46. RENDER OFFSET

```text

measure the output offset

```

```text

SOURCE TIMING

```

```text

OUTPUT COMPENSATION

```



One global encoding offset should not contaminate the underlying beat or event definitions.

\# 50. SOUND DESIGN AUDIT



Before approving a shot:



\## PURPOSE



```text

What does each sound communicate?

Could any cue be removed?

```



\## HIERARCHY



```text

What is the primary sound?

Are secondary cues competing with it?

```



\## TIMING



```text

Is the relevant onset/peak aligned with the relevant visual event?

Was the actual render inspected?

```



\## DENSITY



```text

Is the scene too busy?

Would silence improve the important event?

```



\## MATERIAL



```text

Does the sound feel consistent with the visual object and brand?

```



\## MUSIC



```text

Is music already providing the impact?

Are SFX fighting its rhythm or frequency space?

```



\## LEVEL



```text

Are sounds balanced relative to the actual mix?

Are transient spikes controlled?

```



\## TAIL



```text

Are natural decays preserved where useful?

Are transitions cutting tails awkwardly?

```



\## REPETITION



```text

Are repeated events overly identical?

```



\## PRODUCT TRUTH



```text

Does the sound imply an interaction that does not happen?

```



\## FINAL OUTPUT



```text

Did we verify the exported file?

```

\# 51. SOUND DENSITY AUDIT



If the mix feels crowded:



```text

1\. REMOVE UNNECESSARY CUES

2\. IDENTIFY THE PRIMARY EVENT

3\. REDUCE SECONDARY LAYERS

4\. REDUCE FREQUENCY COMPETITION

5\. CREATE SILENCE

6\. REDUCE LEVELS

7\. ONLY THEN CONSIDER MORE PROCESSING

```



Do not solve clutter by simply lowering everything equally.



\---



\# 52. SOUND REPETITION AUDIT



Across the full film, check:



```text

clicks

impacts

risers

sparkles

notifications

```



Ask:



```text

Are we repeating a sonic pattern because it is meaningful,

```



Maintain a coherent family while varying according to event importance.

\# 53. SOUND SPECTACLE AUDIT

```text

```

\# 54. PRODUCT TRUTH



Sound must respect product truth.



Do not imply:



```text

a notification that does not exist

a success state the product does not show

a mechanical interaction unrelated to the UI

```



Stylization is allowed.



Fabricated product behavior is not.

\# GOLDEN RULE



Sound the event, not the timeline.



Do not add SFX because an animation exists.



Add sound when it improves perception, meaning, rhythm, material, hierarchy, continuity, or emotion.



Synchronize perceptual events rather than arbitrary file starts.



Let important moments be louder than minor moments.



Let silence create contrast.



Let music and SFX cooperate.



And always judge sound against the final picture.
